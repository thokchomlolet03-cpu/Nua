# Synthetic measurement review — teacher construct round 3

## Synthetic status and scope

All findings in this document are **synthetic**. This is a synthetic teacher-role measurement review, not qualified educator review, educational validation, age calibration, or evidence that the lesson measures learning in a real population. The review used only the frozen `lesson.json` and `teacher-construct-round2.md`. No student responses, source code, external services, or external evidence were used.

All 20 frozen question types are retained. The purpose of this round is to make scoring more consistent, separate observable constructs where possible, and reduce false positive and false negative decisions. A fluent answer is not treated as evidence by itself; a short answer is not treated as incorrect by itself.

## Synthetic measurement judgment

The lesson supports a narrow, source-grounded construct: recognizing that a fertilizer comparison is more interpretable when fertilizer is the varied factor, relevant conditions such as light and water are held equal, growth is measured, measurements are repeated, and conclusions remain bounded by the tested conditions.

The 20 labels do not create 20 independent capabilities. Several items repeatedly elicit the same confounding-and-isolation operation. The assessment can therefore provide a breadth inventory, but a total score should not be interpreted as 20 independent dimensions or as a validated measure of general scientific reasoning.

The most defensible scoring unit is an observable component, recorded separately from presentation quality:

| Component | Observable evidence | Do not infer |
|---|---|---|
| Varied factor | Names fertilizer as the intentionally changed factor for this question. | Understanding of all independent-variable terminology. |
| Held-equal conditions | Names light, water, or another relevant condition as equal across groups and connects that control to interpretation. | That unmentioned conditions were actually controlled. |
| Measured outcome | Identifies growth as the recorded outcome and, where requested, a repeatable measurement. | That the learner can design a complete measurement protocol. |
| Isolation logic | States that changing another relevant factor prevents attributing a difference uniquely to fertilizer. | That the learner understands biological causation beyond the passage. |
| Variation logic | States that repeated measurements reveal spread or variation and avoids requiring identical results. | Statistical literacy, sample-size adequacy, or significance testing. |
| Evidence status | Distinguishes a proposed design from observed measurements and labels hypotheses or examples as conditional. | Whether the learner can evaluate data not supplied here. |
| Alternative explanation | Names a plausible competing factor, such as light, water, starting plant differences, or variation, without presenting it as observed fact. | That the named alternative is actually responsible. |
| Scope | Limits the claim to tested plants and conditions and identifies evidence needed for extension. | Transfer to every plant, environment, or fertilizer. |
| Design repair | Proposes a comparison that changes the relevant factor while controlling alternatives. | That the proposed design would be practically adequate in an unprovided setting. |
| Constructed application | Maps the isolation rule to a new context with a varied factor, controls, and measurable outcome. | Broad transfer ability from one open response. |

Score only components requested by an item. A component should receive credit when its meaning is observable in equivalent wording, a labelled table, or a diagram. Terminology such as “independent variable,” “dependent variable,” and “confound” is optional unless the prompt explicitly defines it as the target.

## Handling response forms and difficult cases

### Contradictory answers

Score the proposition relevant to the item, not the most favourable sentence. If an answer says both “fertilizer alone caused the growth” and “light also changed so we cannot isolate fertilizer,” record the contradiction rather than averaging it away. The response should receive credit only for a component that is stated consistently or clearly corrected later. If the final clause explicitly retracts the first claim, annotate “self-correction” and score the corrected position; do not assume that later wording always cancels an earlier claim when the response remains unresolved.

### Fluent but incorrect answers

Fluency, vocabulary, structure, confidence, and length are non-construct evidence in this synthetic review. An answer using “independent variable” and “causal inference” but attributing a difference to fertilizer while light changes fails the isolation component. Score the observable claim and reasoning. Do not award extra credit for technical terminology.

### Short but correct answers

A short answer can earn full credit when it contains every component required by the item. For example, “Change fertilizer only; keep light and water equal, then repeat growth measurements” satisfies the core design components of several items. Do not impose a minimum word count. If a multi-part prompt requires three components and only one is present, score the one present and mark the others missing; do not convert brevity into a global failure.

### Creative or unusual answers

Score functional equivalence. A table, diagram, symbolic notation, analogy, invented but clearly marked scenario, or unconventional example can receive credit when the varied factor, controls, outcome, evidence status, and scope are recoverable. If an analogy is used, score the mapped mechanism, not the imagery. If the mapping is ambiguous, mark the component indeterminate and request clarification rather than treating creativity as error.

### Conditional, hypothetical, and counterfactual answers

Preserve modality. “If fertilizer helps, the fertilizer group may tend to grow more” is a prediction; “the fertilizer group grew more” is an observation claim. “Suppose water differed” must be scored against that altered state, not against the frozen design. A hypothetical example must not be recorded as evidence about the tested plants.

### Answers containing outside knowledge

Outside knowledge may be factually plausible but is not required by this source. Credit it only when it supports the requested component and does not replace source-grounded reasoning. Do not penalize a learner for omitting biology not supplied by the passage. Do not infer mastery of the added knowledge from its presence.

## Synthetic scoring procedure for consistency

1. Parse the response into claims, conditions, evidence statements, and proposed actions; do not score overall impression first.
2. Mark each requested component as **present**, **absent**, **contradicted**, or **indeterminate**.
3. Apply the item-specific rule above. Use equivalent everyday wording and non-text representations.
4. Record evidence-status errors separately from causal-isolation errors. For example, claiming that an unprovided result occurred is different from failing to control light.
5. If a response is contradictory, preserve both propositions and apply the resolution rule above.
6. If the response is too ambiguous to map, record indeterminate and the missing referent; do not infer intent from fluency.
7. Report item scores as component profiles or anchored partial scores. Avoid a single unqualified total across the causal cluster.

For a simple synthetic three-level component scale:

- **0 — not demonstrated:** absent, irrelevant, or contradicted.
- **1 — partial:** relevant idea present but incomplete, weakly connected, or conditionality/scope missing.
- **2 — demonstrated:** requested component is accurate, connected to the scenario, and appropriately qualified.

This scale is a synthetic scoring aid, not a validated rubric. Raters should not use grammar, spelling, accent, handwriting neatness, verbosity, confidence, or advanced vocabulary as component evidence. Accessibility supports that preserve the response meaning should not reduce the score.

## Construct separation and dependency cautions

q1, q7, q9, q11, q14, and q17 share the central confounding/isolation operation. q2, q8, and q19 also reuse the same listed design elements, with q19 adding integration. q12, q13, and q20 share scope and extension reasoning. These items may be retained for breadth, but correlated success should not be described as independent proof of six or three separate abilities.

q3 contains an absent-mechanism demand; q6 contains an abstract prerequisite demand; q18 and q20 are open transfer/inquiry tasks. Their difficulty may reflect wording, background knowledge, or response burden rather than the target construct. q4, q10, q15, and q16 depend especially on distinguishing design information from observations, yet the frozen source supplies no actual data.

If the same learner answers every item fluently by paraphrasing the source, that is a possible false positive for breadth. If a learner understands the principle but uses ordinary language, gives a concise response, or does not know the requested terminology, that is a possible false negative. Component scoring reduces both risks but cannot remove them without richer stimuli and response evidence.

## What cannot be inferred from this frozen round

The following claims are not supported by these materials alone:

- real learning gain, retention, age appropriateness, or instructional effectiveness;
- reliability, rater agreement, item difficulty, discrimination, or fairness across learner groups;
- a learner’s general scientific reasoning, biology knowledge, statistical competence, or ability to run a real experiment;
- whether a learner’s answer reflects independent understanding rather than memorisation, prompting, translation, collaboration, or tool use;
- whether the proposed comparison is practically adequate, ethically appropriate, or biologically controlled beyond the stated scenario;
- an actual fertilizer effect, the magnitude or direction of any effect, statistical significance, or reproducibility;
- causal validity for plants or environments outside the tested conditions;
- that missing details were controlled, that repeated measurements have sufficient sample size, or that growth was measured reliably;
- that an item labelled with a distinct type measures a distinct construct;
- that one response format is equally accessible or equally demanding for all learners.

## Final synthetic conclusion

The frozen assessment can be scored more consistently when each item is decomposed into visible components and modality, evidence status, contradiction, and scope are recorded explicitly. The principal measurement threat is construct repetition: many questions change the surface form while asking whether two changing factors prevent a unique fertilizer conclusion. The principal validity threat is source insufficiency: the one-page passage describes a design but supplies no results, operational measurement details, or extension evidence.

Retain q1–q20 and every frozen type, but interpret results as a synthetic, source-bound profile. Do not infer educator validation, general competence, or real-world causal understanding from fluent or complete-looking answers. **All judgments and recommendations in this document remain synthetic.**
