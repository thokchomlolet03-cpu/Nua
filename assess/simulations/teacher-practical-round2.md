# Synthetic teacher-practical review — round 2

This is a synthetic teacher-role review, not a qualified human educator review and not educational validation. It considers practical use with learners aged 12–16, especially accessibility, pacing, teacher workload and how learner responses could inform follow-up. No students, student fixtures, application code or classroom records were inspected. Therefore, response patterns below are anticipated patterns to monitor, not user research or observed evidence.

## Bottom line

A teacher could prepare and teach the underlying idea, but the current lesson is not yet a low-workload 20-question classroom routine. The passage is brief and conceptually coherent; the question set is broad, largely open-ended, and repeatedly returns to confounding variables, causal attribution and limits of generalisation. Keeping all 20 questions required is workable only if the activity supplies a clear response format, staged sessions, visible success criteria and a lightweight way to classify responses.

The 20+ requirement is preserved. No question is removed, made optional or downgraded. The recommendations below change delivery and follow-up, not the required breadth.

## Synthetic practical judgement by teacher task

### Prepare

Preparation is moderate rather than minimal. A teacher must prepare more than the single-page passage because several prompts require context that is not supplied:

- q10 asks for a predicted pattern without specifying the direction of the hypothetical fertilizer effect.
- q13 refers to a plant that responds differently without clearly stating the bounded claim being tested.
- q18 assumes that learners know enough about a paper helicopter and what “fall time” means to design a comparison.
- q20 refers to “this fertilizer result,” although the lesson supplies no actual growth result.

The repeated anchor is truncated at “fertili” for every question. A teacher can reopen the full passage, but doing so while responding to individual questions increases preparation and in-lesson explanation work. A usable teacher pack would need the complete source passage, a question-specific evidence cue, a short glossary and a response key that accepts equivalent wording.

The teacher also needs to decide in advance what counts as a short answer, labelled table, diagram or explanation. Without that decision, “open response” can become 20 mini-essays and the workload becomes unpredictable.

### Explain

The core explanation is accessible for many learners: one factor changes, relevant conditions stay equal, growth is measured and comparisons are repeated. However, the questions introduce terms and operations at different abstraction levels: variable, mechanism, evidence, assumption, counterfactual, causality, uncertainty, boundaries and synthesis.

For ages 12–16, the teacher should explicitly separate these ideas before independent work:

1. what changes, what stays equal and what is measured;
2. why changing light and fertilizer together prevents a unique fertilizer conclusion;
3. the difference between a proposed design, recorded measurements and a conclusion;
4. the difference between a claim about tested plants and a claim about every plant or environment.

This is a pacing dependency: if “variable,” “comparison,” “evidence” and “cause” are not made concrete first, later questions may measure vocabulary access more than the intended reasoning.

### Pace

Twenty open responses should not be presented as one uninterrupted sitting for this age range. The passage is short, but the response burden is not: many questions require two or three components, and q2, q8, q12, q15, q16, q17, q18, q19 and q20 need justification or an evidence plan.

A practical provisional sequence retains every question:

| Session | Questions | Classroom purpose | Workload control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | q1–q8 | establish definitions, procedure, roles and design comparison | use short definitions, sorting and a labelled design table |
| 2 | q9–q16 | test causal reasoning, uncertainty, counterfactuals and recording | use sentence frames and a shared table rather than eight essays |
| 3 | q17–q20 | compare interpretations, transfer, synthesis and new inquiry | allow paired planning, then collect an individual final response |

This is a synthetic pacing proposal, not a validated timing. A teacher should record actual completion time, number of prompts needing reteaching and unfinished items before adopting a fixed schedule.

Within each session, use a predictable cycle: model one answer, give a constrained response format, allow individual thinking, check one or two answers publicly, then require revision where the reasoning is incomplete. This makes learner responses usable during the lesson without requiring the teacher to give a long written comment on every item.

### Act on responses

The existing rubric names accuracy, claim–evidence connection, perspective relevance and justification, but it does not yet give the teacher a fast action rule. The teacher needs to classify each response by the missing operation, not by fluency alone.

Suggested response codes for live use:

| Code | Response pattern to check | Immediate follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V | roles are confused: fertilizer, light, water or growth are misclassified | return to a four-column “varied / held equal / measured / repeated” table |
| C | learner says fertilizer caused the difference while light or water also changed | ask which other factor could produce the same observation; revise q9 or q14 |
| E | learner treats the design description as proof that growth changed | ask where the measurements are and what data are still missing; revisit q4 or q15 |
| R | learner lists repetition but does not explain variation | compare two hypothetical repeated measurements and ask what repetition contributes; revisit q10 or q19 |
| B | learner generalises to every plant or environment | add the tested conditions and name evidence needed in another setting; revisit q12, q13 or q20 |
| T | transfer answer names a new object but does not specify a changed factor, control or measurement | complete a “change / keep equal / measure” frame for q18 |
| J | answer is relevant but gives a conclusion without the reason or evidence link | require one sentence beginning “This follows only if…” or “This cannot show…” |
| A | access or language barrier is suspected because the learner cannot enter the task, not because the reasoning is demonstrably absent | read the prompt aloud, provide the glossary or sentence frame, and reassess the same operation without lowering the criterion |

These codes are a proposed teacher workflow. They are not diagnostic categories and must not be treated as validated measures of learning.

## Accessibility and age-range needs

The lesson should offer multiple response modes while preserving the reasoning requirement: short written answers, a labelled table, a comparison diagram, oral explanation recorded by the teacher, or sentence-completion frames. A different mode should not remove the requirement to identify the changed factor, controlled conditions, measurement, evidence limit or reasoning link where that is the question criterion.

The teacher should preteach or display plain-language equivalents:

- variable: something that can change;
- isolate an effect: make one possible cause the main difference so alternatives are reduced;
- evidence: information that could support or limit a claim;
- repeated measurement: measuring more than once to examine variation;
- boundary: the conditions or cases the conclusion does not automatically cover.

Accessibility supports should include chunking the passage, highlighting the one-factor/other-conditions structure, reading prompts aloud where appropriate, and separating multi-part prompts into numbered subparts. q3’s biological-mechanism clause should be treated carefully: the source explains comparison logic, not a biological mechanism for fertilizer absorption or growth. A learner should not lose credit for declining to invent that mechanism.

The teacher should also distinguish language production from reasoning. A concise answer with a correct causal limitation may demonstrate the target better than a fluent but unsupported essay. Conversely, a long answer should not receive credit merely for fluency.

## Workload risks and controls

The main workload risk is not lesson preparation alone; it is processing 20 responses with multiple criteria. If every answer receives bespoke prose feedback, the activity is unlikely to be sustainable.

Use a two-level marking routine:

1. During the session, mark only the missing operation using one response code and give a short next-action prompt.
2. After the session, sample one response from each required inquiry type or use a small common checkpoint to judge whether the class can transfer the idea.

Keep an exception list for learners who need individual follow-up. Do not rewrite all 20 answers for all learners. For peer or self-checking, supply observable checks such as “names the one changed factor,” “names at least two equal conditions,” “identifies a measurement,” and “does not claim evidence that was not supplied.”

The teacher should have a one-page tracker with q1–q20 rows and the response codes above. A blank cell means no evidence yet; it must not be interpreted as failure. This protects against confusing non-completion, language/access difficulty and incorrect reasoning.

## Anticipated response patterns meriting follow-up

The following are concrete patterns worth checking in a later synthetic response round. They are hypotheses derived from the prompt demands, not observations:

- learners repeat “keep everything the same” but cannot name light and water or identify fertilizer as the varied factor;
- learners call growth a variable without distinguishing the measured outcome from the deliberately changed condition;
- learners infer that repeated measurements guarantee the fertilizer caused growth, rather than helping reveal variation;
- learners treat a hypothetical prediction, a proposed design and an observed result as interchangeable;
- learners provide a new example for q18 but omit what is held equal or what is measured;
- learners answer q12, q13 or q20 with a universal claim despite the passage’s explicit tested-conditions boundary;
- learners list two factors for q17 but cannot propose a comparison that would distinguish their effects;
- learners give a correct label with no explanation, especially on q3, q8, q16 and q19;
- learners produce a long response that contains the target idea but hides it among unsupported claims;
- learners leave multi-part questions incomplete because the prompt is not visually decomposed.

For each pattern, the follow-up record should contain the exact question ID, the learner response or a privacy-safe excerpt, the missing operation, the support provided and whether the revised response changed. The record must continue to label the data as synthetic and must not be reported as classroom evidence.

## Required follow-up before practical release

Before a teacher-facing release, run the next synthetic assessment round against the actual response fixtures, without changing the 20+ requirement, and report:

- completion and abandonment by question;
- response length or response-mode burden where available;
- frequency of each proposed response code;
- which prompts require clarification or rereading;
- whether the proposed follow-up action produces a more complete second response;
- whether q1–q20 remain distinguishable in the responses rather than only in their labels.

Those results would still be synthetic assessment evidence, not classroom validation. Human classroom review would be needed for claims about accessibility, age suitability, teacher time or educational effectiveness.

## Conclusion

The lesson can preserve all 20 required questions, but practical use requires a staged workflow, explicit response formats, accessibility supports and action codes that turn answers into next steps. The current source supports the central fair-comparison concept; it does not yet support effortless preparation, reliable timing or low-cost interpretation of every open response. Those are synthetic practical findings and design risks, not claims about real teachers or learners.
