# Synthetic construct review — round 2

## Status and scope

This is a **synthetic teacher-role review**, not human educator review, educational validation, or evidence that the assessment is age-calibrated. It is based only on `assess/simulations/lesson.json`. No student responses, previous reviews, source code, or external services were used.

The fixed breadth requirement is respected: all 20 inquiry types are retained. The recommendations below repair prompts, sequence, source support, and scoring rather than delete questions.

## Overall judgment

The lesson supplies a clear core rule: vary one factor, hold relevant conditions equal, measure growth, repeat, and limit conclusions to the tested conditions. It is sufficient for basic recognition and explanation of confounding, control variables, conditional claims, and limited generalisation.

It is not sufficient on its own for several higher-demand claims. The single paragraph contains no actual measurements, no table or graph, no operational definition of growth, no sample size, no randomisation or allocation method, no details about plant comparability, and no concrete example of variation. Consequently, many prompts can be answered by paraphrasing the same sentence. The assessment therefore has nominal breadth but weaker construct differentiation than the 20 labels suggest.

For ages 12–16, the topic and most vocabulary are accessible, but some prompts combine two or three operations, use abstract metacognitive language, or require judging evidence that the source does not provide. Those items need either scaffolding or a richer source packet. Observable criteria should score reasoning components separately, not fluency or length.

## Question-by-question review

| ID / type | Distinct demand actually elicited | Source sufficiency and validity risk | Recommended repair and observable criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| q1 definition | Define a fair test and distinguish it from an uncontrolled comparison. | Sufficient for the intended distinction. “Relevant conditions” remains implicit; students may give a slogan without an example. | Add a two-group mini-scenario with fertilizer, light, and water values. Score: one factor varied, relevant controls identified, and why the uncontrolled comparison cannot isolate a cause. |
| q2 reconstruction | Reorganise the procedure in the learner’s own words. | Sufficient, but heavily overlaps q1, q7, and q8. “Including what stays equal” makes recall of listed details more important than reconstruction. | Place immediately after reading as a low-stakes bridge. Score omission of key design components separately; accept equivalent wording and do not reward verbosity. |
| q3 mechanism | Explain why controls support causal isolation and identify an unexplained biological mechanism. | The isolation mechanism is supported; the biological mechanism is deliberately absent. The second clause may assess science background or guessing rather than the target construct. | Split into two labelled parts or provide a short optional biology note. Score causal-isolation logic and explicit recognition that the passage does not establish fertilizer absorption or plant biology. |
| q4 evidence | Distinguish a confounding claim from actual observations and identify absent data. | Sufficient for noticing that the passage describes a design rather than results. “Which claim” is slightly ambiguous because more than one claim is present. | Quote or number the two relevant statements. Score evidence-type classification, not whether students use the word “confound.” Add a small data-free/design-vs-results distinction before this item. |
| q5 assumptions | Identify the comparability assumption and consequences if it fails. | Mostly sufficient, but “comparability” can mean species, age, health, starting size, or genetic background; no intended level is specified. | Give a bounded list or scenario showing unequal starting size/health. Score assumption, alternative explanation, and conditional consequence; do not treat the possible difference as observed. |
| q6 prerequisites | Explain why knowing “variable” is needed for understanding the design. | Weak construct separation. The source uses the idea but never defines the term; the prompt asks about learning prerequisites rather than applying experimental reasoning. | Provide a one-line glossary or replace the abstract wording with “What must you identify before deciding whether the test changes only fertilizer?” Score identification of varied, controlled, and measured quantities. |
| q7 comparison | Compare a confounded design with an isolating design and select the consequential difference. | Sufficient and useful, but nearly duplicates q9 and q14. “Which difference matters” could refer to light, fertilizer, or the number of factors unless the conclusion is named. | Use paired concrete designs and ask which conclusion each supports. Score comparison linked to causal interpretability, not simple difference listing. |
| q8 classification | Assign independent/controlled/dependent roles to named quantities. | Sufficient for basic classification, but “growth” is an outcome while “light” and “water” may also be measured quantities in real studies. Role depends on the proposed question. | State “for this fertilizer question” and allow a table or drag-sort format. Score role plus justification; accept that fertilizer is the varied explanatory factor and growth the measured outcome. |
| q9 causality | Reject a unique fertilizer attribution when light also changes. | Sufficient, but answer is directly repeated in the passage and overlaps q7/q14/q17. Binary “can” may hide nuanced answers such as joint effects. | Ask for the strongest justified conclusion: “A growth difference is associated with the two changes; fertilizer’s separate effect is not isolated.” Score claim strength and alternative explanation. |
| q10 prediction | Make a conditional prediction across repeated trials while acknowledging variation. | The source supports repetition and variation but gives no direction, scale, or operational measurement. Students may infer that every trial must show growth. | Add a hypothetical baseline and measurement rule, or explicitly accept “a consistent tendency, with some trial-to-trial spread.” Score conditionality, comparison against a baseline/control, and non-absolute language. |
| q11 counterfactual | Update interpretation when a third factor also differs. | Sufficient and age-appropriate. It tests the same confounding rule as q9, with modest additional load. | Use a numerical scenario and require one sentence naming the added alternative explanation. Score correct counterfactual state change and unchanged principle. |
| q12 boundaries | Limit generalisation and specify evidence needed for extension. | The boundary is explicitly supplied, so the first part is recall. Evidence needed for extension is underdetermined without defining target populations and environments. | Provide two new plant/environment cases and ask what must be repeated or varied. Score scope claim, relevant variation in evidence, and no universal conclusion. |
| q13 counterexample | Distinguish a limited claim from a universal claim using a hypothetical exception. | Sufficient, but “responds differently” is vague: direction, magnitude, or no response? It also risks testing formal logic terminology rather than scientific reasoning. | State the candidate claim verbatim and specify the hypothetical difference. Score whether the example is outside the tested scope and whether it refutes only a universal claim. |
| q14 error | Diagnose and correct an invalid causal conclusion. | Sufficient, but largely repeats q9 in error-detection format. “Correct” may invite a bare replacement sentence. | Require: identify the unsupported step, name light as an alternative explanation, and provide the design change. Score diagnosis, corrected claim, and repair—not just the final sentence. |
| q15 uncertainty | Identify missing information needed to judge an actual fertilizer effect. | Strong target, but the source intentionally has no results; without a minimum evidence standard, responses will vary widely. | Supply an evidence checklist or ask students to rank missing items: measured growth, repeated trials, equal controls, starting comparability, and measurement method. Score relevance and distinction between design adequacy and result data. |
| q16 representation | Design a data table and state what recording cannot establish alone. | Source supports recording and limits, but not trial identifiers, units, baseline values, or summary statistics. The table-design portion can become formatting ability. | Provide a blank table frame and ask students to label fields, or permit a verbal schema. Score required variables, repeated observations, units/time point, and causal-limit statement. |
| q17 interpretation | Generate two plausible explanations and a comparison that distinguishes them. | Good differentiation potential, but the source gives only fertilizer and light as explicit alternatives; “two possible interpretations” may reward invented mechanisms. | Constrain alternatives to fertilizer effect, light effect, joint/other-group difference, or random variation, and provide a comparison matrix. Score plausibility, non-commitment, and discriminating design. |
| q18 application | Map the principle to paper-helicopter blade length and identify controls/outcome. | The new context is not in the source, so transfer is genuinely learner-created as the plan notes. However, “relevant” helicopter conditions are open-ended and response burden is high. | Provide a short diagram and fixed question such as flight time. Score one varied factor, at least two justified controls, measurable outcome, and transfer of isolation—not domain trivia. |
| q19 synthesis | Integrate factor isolation and repeated measurement into one coherent explanation. | Sufficient, but it collapses already-tested components and is sensitive to writing fluency. It does not necessarily distinguish synthesis from a longer q2 answer. | Give a causal chain template with two blanks: control isolates attribution; repetition reveals variation. Score explicit relationship between the two functions and appropriate conclusion limits. |
| q20 inquiry | Formulate an answerable generalisation question and an evidence plan. | High demand and under-supported. “Apply elsewhere” leaves population, environment, and evidence design unspecified; it overlaps q12 and is difficult to score consistently. | Require one named new plant/environment and a comparison plan. Score answerability, defined scope, controlled comparison, repeated measurements, and evidence-to-claim fit. |

## Cognitive differentiation

The strongest separations are:

- **Recall/reorganisation:** q2.
- **Concept identification and classification:** q1, q4, q8.
- **Causal diagnosis:** q5, q7, q9, q11, q14, q17.
- **Evidence and uncertainty:** q10, q15, q16.
- **Scope and generalisation:** q12, q13, q20.
- **Transfer and integration:** q3, q18, q19.

The causal-diagnosis cluster is too dense. q7, q9, q11, q14, and q17 vary surface format more than underlying reasoning. This does not make them invalid, but it reduces information about distinct capability. Keep them because breadth is fixed, while differentiating them through concrete stimuli: paired designs, a third confounder, a student error, and a comparison-choice matrix.

## Recommended sequence

1. **Orient and reconstruct:** q2, q1, q8.
2. **Inspect evidence and assumptions:** q4, q5, q6, q15.
3. **Compare and diagnose designs:** q7, q9, q11, q14, q17.
4. **Predict and represent observations:** q10, q16, q19.
5. **Bound conclusions:** q12, q13.
6. **Transfer and generate inquiry:** q3, q18, q20.

This sequence moves from source-grounded operations toward open production. q3 should be split or scaffolded before the final transfer section because its “unexplained biological mechanism” branch is not needed to establish the core construct.

## Source enrichment required for stronger validity

Retain the current paragraph as the rule source, then add a compact evidence packet rather than asking the paragraph to support every demand:

1. A labelled diagram of control, varied, and measured factors.
2. Two or three small designs showing fertilizer-only change, fertilizer-plus-light change, and water also changing.
3. A table of repeated growth measurements with units, starting values, and an explicit measurement rule.
4. A short note defining comparable plants and identifying which comparability features are relevant in the scenario.
5. Two extension cases with a different plant type or environment, clearly marked as hypothetical evidence needs.

The packet should distinguish **design information**, **observed data**, **inference**, and **hypothesis**. That distinction is central to q4, q10, q12, q15, q16, q17, and q20.

## Response burden and scoring controls

Several prompts contain multiple questions. For ages 12–16, use numbered subparts, response limits, and permitted tables/diagrams. A short answer should be able to demonstrate the criterion. Score components such as “varied factor,” “held-equal factor,” “measured outcome,” “alternative explanation,” “evidence status,” and “scope” separately. Do not use grammar, advanced terminology, or answer length as proxies for reasoning.

## Final synthetic conclusion

The assessment is a useful breadth inventory of fair-comparison reasoning, but it is not yet a well-differentiated 20-construct measure. Its central construct is repeatedly elicited, while several higher-order items exceed the information supplied by the one-page source. Preserve all 20 inquiry types, enrich the source with concrete designs and data, sequence from reconstruction to open inquiry, and make each scoring criterion observable and component-based. These recommendations improve synthetic assessment quality; they do not constitute human educational validation.
